*Anicca*, *dukkha*, and *anattā*. These are the three marks of existence, as taught by
Shakyamuni Buddha. That is, *anicca* impermenance, *dukkha* suffering, and *anattā* the
lack of any self-sustaining essence.

Why not address him as just the Buddha? Shakyamuni is the Buddha, right? Technically,
there are many Buddhas, infinitely many, because of *samsāra*. In Theravāda Buddism, 28
of them are named, the last being Gotama/Gautama, and it is predicted that one is to
appear in the future, named Metteya/Maitreya. The point is: there is a lot of theology,
cosmology, and soteriology associated with Buddhism (unlike misconceptions I had
carried), and there are different schools of Buddhism, each differing in this and that.
For instance, in Theravāda tradition, the Buddha is neither a supreme god, a deva, nor a
man, whereas in Mahāyāna Buddhism, the Buddha is transcendent, omniscient, spiritually
omnipotent, and much more.[^LotusSutta] There are also views of the Buddha being simply
nothing more than an insightful wise person. This is the view I prefer. There are many
things I find myself contemplating that are strewn all over the Buddhist corpus, like how
the fruits of a tree lie. I find it the most significant of them the term *tathatā*,
which is Pali, roughly translated as thusness or suchness.


## Tathatā is not Noumenon

Now, how do you intuit or just think about this concept of tathatā? Well, first, let us
bring in the Kantian term *noumenon*. Noumenon is the world as it is, detached
from the idiosyncrasies of any sensory perception. Consider an object, any object, say a
cup, that is existing with no observers at all whatsoever, to see it, hear it, smell it,
taste it, or to feel it. The attributes (human attributes, that is, which we endow it
with) such as colour, shape, smell, taste, or sound, does not exist in this scenario. The
cup is as it is. This is the *noumenon* of the cup. Whereas when an observer exists, how
the observer observes the cup is the *phenomenon* of the cup. This phenomenon is called
*dhamma*. Still we have not quite made clear what tathatā is. But what we are making
clear is that tathatā is not noumenon. Someone who is familiar with noumenon, hearing
tathatā might naturally think so (like me), but no.

What do I mean by tathatā? It is the quintessence of reality, where nothing has an
intrinsic existence (*suññatā*); it is both everything and nothing; it is the aspect of
simply being, where everything is equally significant, therefore equally insignificant;
it is also the awareness of suññatā. That is how it can be described, but I believe it is
still very renitent to intuition. Let us look at it like this.

  1. Everything’s connected, man.
  2. Everything’s about me. I’m so fucking important, right?
  3. We’re just outcomes of chance.
  4. Is a human life more valuable than, say, an ant?

Think about these four statements together. Sit on it. Does an idea form, or an outline,
a silhouette? Tatathā can be surmised as that, but it is still a simplification. It is
one of those ideas that the more you entertain it, fight it, the more and more clearer it
becomes. I know I have been brandishing too many terms here, but it is a core idea of
what I am trying to demonstrate. We go on to the next one.


## Distilling Samsāra

The term *samsāra* has a unanimous clear meaning in Buddhism. It is the cyclicity of
life, death, rebirth, and redeath, without beginning (*anamatagga*) and without end
(*ananta*), laden with suffering (*dukkha*), and the salvation from dukkha is *nibbāna*.
This is very far from what I mean by samsāra, which is more grounded in the
existentialist and absurdist sense, unlike the original sense which is, honestly, too
much. Take a look at this, for reference.

Samsāra](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/74/The_wheel_of_life%2C_Buddhism_Bhavachakra.jpg)

I take it to mean the cyclicity inherent in life, the repetitive tendency of the world we
often do not observe, where we do things again and again, hacking away at our life hoping
that it bends to how we wish it be. The important connotation here is the futility of
wanting control in life and that repetition seems to be the norm; you might note that
this want is due to a blindness to tathatā.


## Thereby Meeting the Absurd

Anicca and anattā, collate them together with Absurdism, you find they are a constituent
of the Absurd (due leeway for such a phrasing) especially anattā. Camus introduces *the
Absurd* as the conflict between a person’s want for purpose and the world’s stark lack of
it. This is the central point of Absurdism. The world is unbound by human rationality.
Have you ever committed an act of noble intention that bore disastrous, sometimes
diametric, outcomes? Were you amused by that turn of events? Ever thought why your
intention appeared to have no practical value?  If you have ever trodden that trail, you
have passed by the Absurd, and perhaps you could not then quite make out what you had
come across. But when you look at samsāra through the lens of tathatā, you meet it, you
confront it, you come to terms with the Absurd. You never resolve it, you only come to
terms with it. Therefore I argue that tathatā is a good response to the Absurd. And that
is why, I think, like I made clear, it is rather evasive to simple definitions.


[^LotusSutta]: In the *Lotus Sutta*, a venerated Mahāyāna sutta, the lifespan of a Buddha
is immeasurable. It also describes the Buddha achieved *nibbana* or *nirvana* many
*kappas/kalpas* (aeons) ago, and that he teaches *dhamma* through his manifestations.
